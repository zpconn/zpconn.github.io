Beautiful problems: the first example

March 11, 2016

The following problem appeared on the 2016 Team Selection Test as part of the process of identifying the contestants who will represent the United States in the IMO (International Math Olympiad).

Problem

Let pp be a prime number, and let 𝔽p\mathbb{F}_p represent the finite field of integers modulo pp. Let 𝔽p[x]\mathbb{F}_p[x] be the set of polynomials with coefficients drawn from 𝔽p\mathbb{F}_p, and define the function Φ:𝔽p[x]→𝔽p[x]\Phi : \mathbb{F}_p[x]\rightarrow \mathbb{F}_p[x] via

Φ(P)=∑i=0naixpi\Phi(P) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^{p^i}

whenever P(x)=∑i=0naixiP(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^i. Prove that for all nonzero polynomials F,G∈𝔽p[x]F,G\in \mathbb{F}_p[x], one has Φ(gcd(F,G))=gcd(Φ(F),Φ(G))\Phi(\text{gcd}(F,G)) = \text{gcd}(\Phi(F),\Phi(G)). (The gcd of two polynomials F,GF,G is the monic polynomial of greatest degree that divides both FF and GG.)

Solution

The solution presented here relies on the technique of invariants. Specifically, we present a special operation that can only be applied a finite number of times and which keeps a certain equality invariant throughout all its applications. This produces a finite chain of equalities which collapse to the desired result.

We make a few preliminary observations that follow from the familiar identity (a+b)p=ap+bp(a+b)^p = a^p+b^p which holds in the field of pp elements. Specifically, one has Φ(xP)=Φ(P)p\Phi(xP) = \Phi(P)^p and Φ(F+G)=Φ(F)+Φ(G)\Phi(F+G)=\Phi(F)+\Phi(G).

Suppose F,G∈𝔽p[x]F,G\in \mathbb{F}_p[x] are distinct monic polynomials, and suppose without loss of generality that deg(F)≤deg(G)\text{deg}(F) \leq \text{deg}(G). Define a reduction on the pair (F,G)(F,G) to be the following operation:

  1. Compute c=deg(G)−deg(F)c = \text{deg}(G) - \text{deg}(F).
  2. Replace the pair (F,G)(F,G) with the new pair (F,G−xcF)(F,G - x^c F).
  3. Make each polynomial in the new pair monic by dividing out by its leading coefficient.

Observe that a reduction maintains the gcd of the pair both before and after the operation. Each reduction lowers the degree of the larger polynomial, so after finitely many reductions we reach a pair of the form (D,xcD)(D, x^c D), where the next reduction would produce zero. Since the gcd never changes, D=gcd(F,G)D=\text{gcd}(F,G).

Define the Φ\Phi-pair of the pair (F,G)(F,G) to be (Φ(F),Φ(G))(\Phi(F),\Phi(G)). A reduction on (F,G)(F,G) sends its Φ\Phi-pair to the Φ\Phi-pair of (F,G−xcF)(F,G-x^c F), specifically (Φ(F),Φ(G)−Φ(F)pc)(\Phi(F), \Phi(G) - \Phi(F)^{p^c}) thanks to the properties of Φ\Phi mentioned above. Thus the gcd of the corresponding Φ\Phi-pair is preserved as well.

We run the reduction process for (F,G)(F,G) and also take note of the sequence of Φ\Phi-pairs thus produced. The sequence of Φ\Phi-pairs ends at the Φ\Phi-pair of (D,xcD)(D, x^c D), which is (Φ(D),Φ(D)pc)(\Phi(D), \Phi(D)^{p^c}), and its gcd is Φ(D)\Phi(D). Thus,

gcd(Φ(F),Φ(G))=Φ(D)=Φ(gcd(F,G)),\text{gcd}(\Phi(F),\Phi(G)) = \Phi(D) = \Phi(\text{gcd}(F,G)),

as desired.
